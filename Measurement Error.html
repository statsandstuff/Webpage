Measurement Error

“a rose by any other name would smell as sweet”

- Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet

Before we begin, a note on nomenclature:

Consider the usual “causal equation” or “structural equation” or “population regression function” or whatever else you want to call it,

\[ \begin{align*} y_i = x_i\beta + \epsilon_i. \end{align*} \]

In the econometrics literature it is typical to refer to the variable \[y_i\] as the “dependent variable”. In the statistics literature it is more common to call this same variable the “response variable” or even the “outcome variable”. Similarly, for the variable \[x_i\] your friendly neighbourhood econometrician might call this the “explanatory variable” or the “covariate(s)” or the “independent variable”. Your local data-scientist might be more likely to call it the “regressor” or the set of “features”, while your distinguished statistician friend might call it the “design matrix”.

All of these naming conventions are valid and developed over the years of various reasons. In my experience, I have found that the econometricians seem to have the best treatments and discussions about measurement error, and since this piece is about measurement error, we will adopt the naming conventions of the econometricians for today. Please direct all criticisms of this decision to your nearest paper waste bin.

Measurement Error in the Independent Variable

First, we shall discuss measurement error in the independent variable, that is to say that you thought you were measuring some variable, \(X_i\) (say, illicit drug habits from a survey, or self-assessed health outcomes, or even taxable incomes) , but what you actually recorded was

\[ \begin{align} \tilde{X}_i = X_i + W_i \end{align} \tag{1}\]

where \(W_i\) represents the perturbation that causes what you measured to not be a fully accurate measurement of the underlying variable of interest.

If our causal model is given by

\[ \begin{align} Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_i + U_i \end{align} \]

where \(U_i\) is the usual econometric disturbance term (that is, \(U_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\)), then we can rewrite this causal model in terms of $\tilde{X}i$$ (the variable that we actually measured) rather than \(X_i\) (the variable that we were trying to measure). This gives us

\[ \begin{align*} Y_i &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \tilde{X}_i + \left[\beta_1(X_i - \tilde{X}_i) + U_i\right] \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \tilde{X}_i + V_i \end{align*} \]

where \(V_i = \beta_1 \left(X_i - \tilde{X}i\right) + U_i\).

Classical Error in Variables (CEV)

One kind of measurement error that we run into is called Classical Error in Variables (CEV) or sometimes Classical Measurement Error. This is the case that occurs when we assume that

\[ W_i \sim (0, \sigma^2_W). \] On average, the size of the “disturbance” component in Equation Equation 1 is \(0\), and it comes from some distribution with constant, finite variance. Additionally, we assume that

\[ \rho(W_i, X_i) = 0 \]
that is, there is zero correlation between the size of the measurement error and the size of the underlying variable, and we also assume that

\[ \rho(W_i, U_i) = 0 \]that is, there is zero correlation between the size of the measurement error and the econometric disturbance term (and hence there is no correlation between the size of the measurement error and the independent variable, \[y_i.\]

Essentially, random noise is being added to the measurements of \(X_i\).

While it may be tempting to think that random noise will “cancel each other out” and lead to no effect, this is not the case. CEV measurement error still has some rather nasty effects on our OLS estimator. Specifically, it leads to a thing called attenuation bias.

Attenuation Bias

\[ |\hat{\beta}_1| < |\beta_1|. \]

The OLS estimate for the slope coefficient (that is, the effect of \(X_i\) on \(Y_i\) will be dragged towards zero (no effect) under CEV measurement error.

Proof:
In the regression given by the conditional expectation function

\[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[Y_i | X_i\right] &= \mathbb{E}\left[\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + U_i\right | X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[\beta_0 + \beta_1X_i\right] \end{align*} \]

OLS calculates the sample estimate as

\[ \hat{Y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1X_i \]

where \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{Cov(X_i, Y_i)}{Var(X_i)}\). If we rearrange Equation Equation 1 we can see that

\[ X_i = \tilde{X_i} - W_i \]

and therefore

\[ \begin{align*}Var(X_i) &= Var\left(\tilde{X}_i - W_i\right) \\&= Var(\tilde{X}_i) + Var(W_i)\quad \text{since Var is additive when the correlation between the RVs is } 0. \\&= \sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_W\end{align*} \]

where \(\sigma^2_X\) is the population variance of the true underlying explanatory variable of interest, \(X_i\).

We can also see that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y_i) &= \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + U_i) \\ &= \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, \beta_1X_i + U_i) &&\quad \text{since covariance with a constant is zero} \\ &= \beta_1 \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_i) + \operatorname{Cov}(X_i,U_i) &&\quad \text{by linearity of covariance}\\ &= \beta_1 \sigma^2_X &&\quad \text{since } Cov(X_i, U_i) = 0 \text{ by assumption}. \end{align*} \] Combining these two terms we can see that

\[ \begin{align*} \hat{\beta}_1 &= \frac{Cov(X_i, Y_i)}{Var(X_i)} \\ &= \frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_W} \beta_1.\\ \square \end{align*} \]

Whenever $\sigma^2_W > 0$ (that is, there is a non-zero measurement error component), then we have $|\hat{\beta}1| < |\beta_1|$.

As \(\sigma^2_W \rightarrow \infty\)$, we can see that \(\hat{\beta}_1 \rightarrow 0\).

As \(\sigma^2_W \rightarrow 0\), we can see that \(\hat{\beta}_1 \rightarrow \beta_1\).

The becomes a little bit more involved when there are multiple regressors, and even more so when some of them are mismeasured while others are not. Nonetheless, the key finding of attenuation bias in the coefficient estimate of the mismeasured explanatory variable remains.

Best Guess Measurement Error

Best guess measurement error is a very particular kind of measurement error that actually does not lead to any bias in the OLS estimator. Whether or not it is reasonable to assume best guess measurement error in reality is a different story….

Again, let us assume that $\tilde{X_i} = X_i + W_i$. We say that $\tilde{X}_i$ represents individual \(i\)’s “best guess” about $X_i$. That is,

\[ \tilde{X}_i = \mathbb{E}[X_i | I_i] \]

where $I_i$ is the set of all relevant information available to the individual.

First, we note that

\[ E[W_i | I_i] = 0. \]

Proof:

\[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[W_i | I_i] &= \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i - X_i | I_i] &&\quad \text{using the definition of } W_i \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i | I_i] - \mathbb{E}[X_i | I_i] &&\quad \text{by linearity of expectation} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}(X_i | I_i)|I_i] - \tilde{X}_i &&\quad \text{by definition}\\ &= \mathbb{E}[X_i | I_i] - \tilde{X}_i \\ &= \tilde{X}_i - \tilde{X}_i \\ &= 0.\\ \square \end{align*} \]

Again, we know that the OLS estimator is given by

\[ \begin{align*} \hat{\beta}_1 &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, Y_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)} \\ &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, \beta_0 + \beta_1X_i + U_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)} &&\quad \text{using the structural equation of } Y_i\\ &= \frac{\beta_1 \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, X_i) + \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, U_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)} &&\quad \text{by linearity of } \operatorname{Cov}. \end{align*} \]

First, let us consider \(\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}i, Xi)\). We can see that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, X_i) &= \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, \tilde{X}_i - W_i) &&\quad \text{by definition of } \tilde{X}_i \\ &= \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, \tilde{X}_i) - \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, W_i) &&\quad \text{by the linearity of } \operatorname{Covariance} \\ \end{align*} \]

but we can know that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, W_i) &= \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i W_i] + \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i]\mathbb{E}[W_i] &&\quad \text{by definition of Covariance} \\ &= \mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_iW_i | I_i])+ \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i]\mathbb{E}[W_i] &&\quad \text{using the Law of Iterated Expectations} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i \underbrace{\mathbb{E}(W_i| I_i)}_{=0}] + \mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_i]\mathbb{E}\left[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}(W_i|I_i)}_{=0}\right] &&\quad \text{using the Law of Iterated Expectations again} \\ &= 0, \end{align*} \]

which allows us to claim that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, X_i) &= \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, \tilde{X}_i) \\ &= \operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i.) \end{align*} \]

Next, we consider \(\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}i, U_i)\). Along with the usual assumption that the econometric disturbance term is exogenous to the explanatory variables, we also assume that the information set, \(I_i\), tells us nothing about the disturbance term. That is, \(\mathbb{E}[U_i | I_i] = \mathbb{E}[U_i] = 0\).

Hence, we can see that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, U_i) &= \mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_i, U_i) + \mathbb{E}(X_i)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}(U_i)}_{=0} \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_i U_i | I_i)\right] &&\quad \text{by the Law of Iterated Expectations} \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_i \underbrace{\mathbb{E}(U_i | I_i)}_{=0}\right] \\ &= 0. \end{align*} \] Thus, we are now able to claim that

\[ \begin{align*} \hat{\beta}_1 &= \frac{\beta_1 \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, X_i) + \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{X}_i, U_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)} \\ &= \frac{\beta_1 \operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_i)} \\ &= \beta_1. \end{align*} \]

Clearly, there is no bias — attenuation or otherwise — in this special case of “best guess” measurement error.

Systematic Misreporting

The case of systematic misreporting is rather broad and general, and thus our discussion here will be equally as broad and general. In the case that individuals are systematically misreporting, that is to say that the noise measure, \(\tilde{X}_i\)is consistently larger/smaller than the true value \(X_i\) then the OLS estimator will be a biased estimator for the population slope coefficient. This is more likely to occur in settings with survey data where there is an inherent incentive for respondents to lie in a particular direction. For example, perhaps respondents may wish to lie about their income and consistently under report for tax reasons. Perhaps respondents are being asked about how often they partake in illegal activities; there is an obvious incentive for them to lie for fear of legal ramifications.

Systematically misreporting tends to introduce a directional bias in the direct of the misreport. We will not go into any specific details here, as there are a large number of minutiae relating to the specific context and setting that make the investigation generally not worth pursuing for a generalist audience.

Measurement Error in the Dependent Variable

Now we consider what happens when the measurement error is not in the \(X_i\) variable, but rather the \(Y_i\) variable.

Consider the structural equation

\[ \begin{align*} y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + ... + \beta_k x_{ki} + u_i \end{align*} \]

where the econometric disturbance term $u_i$ satisfies the usual Gauss-Markov assumptions such that \(\mathbb{E}(u_i | x_i) = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}(u_i x_i) = 0\), where \(u_i\) comes from a population distribution with some constant and finite variance denoted by \(\sigma^2\).

Instead of being able to observe/measure \(y_i\), we may only be able to observe/measure \(\tilde{y}_i = y_i + W_i\) so that, again, \(W_i\) represents the measurement error causing component. Sometimes we refer to \(y_i\) as a “latent variable”.

We can rewrite the structural equation in terms of the measured variable,

\[ \begin{align*} \tilde{y}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{1i} + ... + \beta_k x_{ki} + \underbrace{v_i}_{v_i \equiv u_i + w_i}. \end{align*} \]

We know that the OLS estimator is an unbiased and consistent estimator whenever the Gauss-Markov assumptions hold. Specifically, for the structural equation rewritten as above, we require that \(\mathbb{E}[v_i | x_i] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[v_i x_i] = 0\). When might this be true? When might this be violated?

We can see that

\[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[v_i | x_i] &= \mathbb{E}[u_i + w_i | x_i] \\ &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[u_i | x_i]}_{=0} + \mathbb{E}[w_i | x_i] &&\quad \text{by linearity of expectaion} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[w_i | x_i]. \end{align*} \]

If the measurement error is statistically independent with the explanatory variables and has mean zero, then the Gauss Markov assumptions will hold. If instead there is statistical independence but a non-zero mean, then the intercept term, \(\beta_0\), will be biased but the slope coefficients will be unaffected. This is usually not much of a concern (unless for some reason we really want to estimate a causal effect of…an intercept?).

So, statistical independence between \(w_i\) and \(x_i\) is the necessary condition for \(\mathbb{E}[v_i | x_i] = 0\). As a corollary of this statistical independence, we also have

\[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[v_i x_i] &= \mathbb{E}[(u_i + w_i) x_i] \\ &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[u_i x_i]}_{=0} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[w_i x_i]}_{=0} \\ &= 0. \end{align*} \]So, the Gauss-Markov assumptions are satisfied and OLS gives an unbiased and consistent estimator, when there is statistical independence between \(w_i\) and \(x_i\). As an implication of this, the usual OLS regression model inference is also valid.

It’s not all sunshine and rainbows though. Notice that

\[ \begin{align*} \operatorname{Var}(v_i) &= \operatorname{Var}(u_i + w_i) \\ &= \sigma^2 + \sigma^2_w \\ &> \sigma^2. \end{align*} \]

The implication of this is that measurement error in the dependent variable leads to inflated standard errors for your OLS estimators.

To summarise:

  • Measurement error in the dependent variable can only cause bias in the OLS estimator if it is systematically related to an explanatory variable.

  • Measurement error in the dependent vartiable due to random noise that is statistically independent with the explanatory variables does not invalidate the OLS estimator. This is not the case when the measurement error is in the independent variable(s) such as in CEV measurement error which does bias the OLS estimator (attenuation bias).
